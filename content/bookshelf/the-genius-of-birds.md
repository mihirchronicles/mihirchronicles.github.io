## II. Big Ideas
- Ackerman explores classic examples of the exceptional intelligence of parrots, as well as lesser known instances of aviary acumen found in less exotic birds such as chickadees and sparrows.
- Ackerman’s definition of genius is flexibility in meeting environmental and social challenges. That’s a lower bar than IQ.
- Intelligence comes in many forms. Social, vocal, spatial, technical, and aesthetic intelligence are separate skills. Ackerman treats them as “evolutionary stepping stones” for solving problems in the natural world.
- Birds are individuals. Some get bored quickly, some take risks that harm them, and some are committed to their partners.
- Being clever doesn’t guarantee survival. Intelligence does not automatically improve a species’ survival in new or unstable environments. Bold birds explore fast but can trade away accuracy.
- An underlying question runs through the whole book: do we judge birds by standards that suit our own needs rather than theirs?
- Birds have been around much longer than we have, and are not driving the sixth mass extinction, which complicates our claim to be the smart ones. Their brilliance doesn’t protect them from our choices. Protecting it means protecting habitat: the trees, the canopy, the upslope refuges.
- Ackerman closes by asking whether we will accept a diminished world of sparrow-like generalists or work to preserve avian diversity, arguing every species has its own form of genius.
- Corvids and parrots rank most innovative; quails, ostriches, and turkeys rank lowest. Bird brains are dense. Corvids and parrots place nearly 80 percent of neurons in cortex-like structures. 
- Green-backed Herons used insects as bait to catch fish, and vultures in Zimbabwe waited on fences near minefields for animals to set off the explosives. 
- Science spent about a century assuming birds couldn’t think in complex ways because they lack a layered neocortex. The fix came when researchers showed birds have a cortex-like structure, the dorsal ventricular ridge, organized in clusters rather than layers.
- Relative size matters more than absolute size. An elephant’s brain is under 1% of its body mass, while a New Caledonian crow’s is about 3.75%.
- City crows adapt too: carrion crows in Japan were filmed placing nuts in crosswalks, letting traffic crush them, then retrieving them when the light changed.
- American crows recognize individual human faces, remember dangerous people for at least nine years, and share that information.
- Zebra finch song learning parallels human speech: tutor, mental template, babbling, and millions of practice repetitions.
- A 2014 genome project across 48 species found over 50 genes active in both singing songbirds and speaking humans.
- Songs can be very old. A Swamp Sparrow song lineage has been passed down almost unchanged for about 1,500 years.
- Bowerbirds are described as the only animals besides humans known to use objects in elaborate displays to attract mates. They create optical illusions by placing large items at the back of the bower and small ones at the front, which makes the space look bigger.
- Some birds use plants as medicine, lining nests with herbs like yarrow, apple mint, and lavender, which may protect chicks from bacteria and parasites.
- White-crowned sparrows captured in Seattle and released about 2,300 miles away in New Jersey quickly reoriented toward their wintering grounds.
- Clark’s nutcrackers cache up to 33,000 seeds and recover them months later with about 70 percent accuracy. These birds also plant forests: nutcracker seeds they never recover can become new pines.
- The Arctic Tern makes a round trip of nearly 44,000 miles each year.
- The house sparrow spread from 16 birds introduced to Brooklyn in 1851 to every continent except Antarctica.
- The trend is that generalists gain while specialists decline.
